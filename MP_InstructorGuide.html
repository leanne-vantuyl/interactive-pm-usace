Course Structure

PROSPECT 355 is a 4-day Project Management course for USACE project managers. All class time is reserved for application, case-file work, and discussion. As MP PM Lead, you lead the Military Programs lessons and co-lead the Foundation Block. The course follows one PM and one project: Maria Chen managing Building 4521 Renovation.

Your Opening

Introduce yourself as the Military Programs PM lead. Ground the course in the case study: Maria Chen is managing Building 4521 Renovation -- the same project, but you bring the Military Programs lens to it. Same PM lifecycle, different program context.

Hit three anchors: (1) Military Programs projects operate under program-specific funding authorities -- constraints are real and non-negotiable. (2) The military customer is a critical stakeholder with requirements authority. (3) Procurement method shapes the PM approach -- know your contract before you manage it.

Case File Introduction
  • Distribute Building 4521 case file packets
  • Walk the scenario overview: Building 4521 Renovation, Maria Chen as PM
  • Explain how both case files run in parallel all week
  • Ask: "What program authorities apply here? What does that change about how Maria manages the project?"
Co-Lead Coordination

Coordinate the Foundation Block split with CW lead before Day 1. CW typically takes PM Framework and lifecycle overview; you take Military Programs context and program-specific authorities. Both alternate on the shared lifecycle, drawing from Building 4521 throughout.

Learning Objectives
  • Develop a scope statement that captures both functional requirements and exclusions
  • Apply MILCON scope boundary discipline -- what's in the DD Form 1391 scope
  • Distinguish scope creep from scope growth; know which requires authorization change
CF02 Facilitation

Teams draft a scope statement for Building 4521 using the approved project description as the boundary. Then: introduce a scope change scenario (the sponsor requests an addition). Walk through: Is this a scope change? Who has approval authority? What is the process?

Your garrison customer says "while you're there, can you add..." How do you respond, and what's your decision process?
Key Teaching Points
  • Scope = what's authorized: Scope is defined by the approved project description; changes must go through the formal change process
  • Scope creep vs. scope growth: Creep is unauthorized; growth is re-authorized -- both require PM discipline to manage
  • Scope statement components: Product scope (deliverables), project scope (work), exclusions, constraints, assumptions
  • PMBOK ref: Ch. 7 Scope; EM 5-1-11 scope management guidance
Watch For MILCON PMs often confuse scope (what we're building) with requirements (what it needs to do). Keep these separate -- requirements drive scope, not the reverse.
Transition to L3 (CW): "Scope defines what. [CW lead] will show you how to decompose that into a Work Breakdown Structure."

Learning Objectives
  • Build a network diagram from the Building 4521 WBS
  • Identify the critical path and calculate float for key activities
  • Apply schedule compression techniques appropriate to MILCON
CF04 Facilitation

Give teams the Building 4521 WBS from L3 (CW). Ask them to sequence 10-15 key activities, draw the network, and identify the critical path. Focus: Where is float? Where do you have zero tolerance for delay?

Then introduce a scenario: a critical long-lead item (structural steel) is delayed 3 weeks. What's the impact? What are your options?

The contractor says the critical path is 2 weeks longer than your contract schedule. What do you do, and in what sequence?
Key Teaching Points
  • Critical path: Longest path through the network -- any delay here delays project completion
  • Float types: Total float (network), free float (activity) -- different implications for schedule decisions
  • Compression options: Fast-tracking (parallel work), crashing (more resources) -- both have cost and risk tradeoffs
  • MILCON schedule drivers: Military construction season, Beneficial Occupancy Date (BOD), garrison operational needs
Instructor Tip Ask: "What's the difference between the schedule baseline and the working schedule?" PMs need to manage both -- the baseline for contract performance, the working schedule for day-to-day decisions.
Transition to L5 (CW): "Schedule is your time baseline. [CW lead] will close out the planning phase with cost estimating."

Learning Objectives
  • Calculate SV, CV, SPI, and CPI from Building 4521 performance data
  • Develop an EAC forecast and explain the assumptions behind it
  • Communicate EVM status to a non-technical stakeholder in plain language
CF07 Facilitation

Provide teams with Month 6 performance data for Building 4521 (PV, EV, AC). Teams calculate the core metrics and develop an EAC using at least two methods. Then: "How would you brief these numbers to the project sponsor in 60 seconds?"

SV = EV - PV  |  CV = EV - AC
SPI = EV / PV  |  CPI = EV / AC
EAC = BAC / CPI (if past performance continues)
EAC = AC + (BAC - EV) (if remaining work at plan)
Your CPI is 0.87 at Month 6. What does that tell you, what doesn't it tell you, and what's your next action?
Key Teaching Points
  • EVM is a forecasting tool, not just a report: CPI/SPI trends matter more than point-in-time values
  • MILCON EVM thresholds: Know your reporting requirements -- when does the PM have to escalate?
  • CPI below 1.0: Spending more than earned; EAC likely exceeds BAC
  • SPI below 1.0: Accomplishing less than planned; schedule impact depends on critical path
Watch For Groups often focus on the math and miss the management decision. Keep redirecting: "OK, you have a CPI of 0.87 -- so what are you going to DO about it?"
Instructor Tip Have at least one pre-computed worked example ready. If a team makes an arithmetic error, it derails the discussion -- give them the right numbers and move to the analysis.
Transition to L8 (CW): "EVM shows you where you are. [CW lead] will work through what might still happen -- risk management."

Learning Objectives
  • Process a contract change request through the integrated change control process
  • Distinguish constructive changes from formal modifications
  • Apply MILCON change authority thresholds
CF10 Facilitation

Present a Building 4521 change scenario: contractor identifies a differing site condition (unforeseen underground utilities). Teams walk through: (1) Is this a valid change claim? (2) What's the process? (3) What's the impact analysis? (4) Who approves?

Add complexity: the contractor continues work without written direction. What's your exposure?

At what point does a verbal direction from the PM create a constructive change? How do you protect the government while keeping the project moving?
Key Teaching Points
  • Integrated change control: Every change goes through a defined process -- impacts on cost, schedule, scope, risk must all be assessed
  • Constructive change: Any government action that changes the work without a formal modification -- creates liability
  • MILCON thresholds: Changes above the simplified acquisition threshold or percentage thresholds require specific approvals
  • Documentation discipline: In MILCON, if it isn't written, it didn't happen -- protect yourself and the government
Watch For PMs often want to "just get the work done" without formal change processing. This lesson is where you address that habit directly -- the change control process protects both sides.
Transition to L11 (CW): "Change management is reactive discipline. [CW lead] will flip that -- how do you proactively turn uncertainty into opportunity?"

Learning Objectives
  • Execute a structured project closeout for Building 4521
  • Conduct a lessons-learned session that generates actionable knowledge
  • Complete administrative and contractual closeout requirements
CF13 Facilitation

This is the capstone -- teams formally close both Building 4521 and Building 4521 case files. For Building 4521: complete the closeout checklist (contract closeout, final payments, as-built documentation, real property records, lessons learned). Then run a structured after-action: What went well? What would you do differently? What do we capture for the next PM?

Close the entire course: What's the one thing you're taking back to your project?

Lessons learned are filed and forgotten in most organizations. What would have to change about how we capture and share them for the knowledge to actually transfer to the next project?
Key Teaching Points
  • Closing is not just paperwork: Project closure releases resources, formally ends obligations, and transfers knowledge
  • MILCON closeout: Real property records, as-built drawings, warranty documentation, contractor past performance evaluation
  • Lessons learned: Must be specific and actionable -- not "communicate better" but "hold weekly PDT sync at project initiation"
  • PM professional obligation: You owe the next PM the institutional knowledge you gained
Course Closing L13 is both the last MP lesson and the course capstone. Build in time for the room to reflect on the full 4-day arc. This moment earns the most when participants connect what they practiced to what they'll do Monday morning.
Transition to Action Planning: "Four days. Two case files. One job: bring it back. Take the next 20 minutes to write three specific things you will do differently when you get back to your project."
