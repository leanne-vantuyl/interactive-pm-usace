Course Structure

PROSPECT 355 is a 4-day Project Management course for USACE project managers. All class time is reserved for application, case-file work, and discussion. Doctrine and process live in the pre-work read-aheads. As CW PM Lead, you lead the Civil Works PM lessons and co-lead the Foundation Block. The course follows one PM and one project: Maria Chen managing Building 4521 Renovation.

Your Opening

Introduce yourself as the Civil Works PM lead. Ground the course in the case study: Maria Chen is a USACE PM managing Building 4521 Renovation from charter through closeout. Every case file this week is a chapter from her project.

Hit three anchors: (1) USACE PM is relationship-based, not just process-based. (2) Every case file is a real decision point Maria faces -- the doctrine gives you the framework, the case gives you the pressure. (3) PMBOK 8th Ed and EM 5-1-11 are the authoritative sources -- you apply them, not memorize them.

Learning Objectives
  • Identify the right stakeholders for the Building 4521 PDT
  • Draft a project charter that captures scope boundaries and authorities
  • Apply EM 5-1-11 Chapter 2 guidance to the Building 4521 scenario
CF01 Facilitation

Open with: "You've read the doctrine. Now you're the PM on Building 4521. Who's on your PDT and why?" Give 5 minutes of table work first, then call on groups.

Push beyond the obvious stakeholders. Surface: Real Estate, Environmental, Cost Engineering, Contracting. Add Operations and the facility user community.

What authority does the PM have at charter signing that they won't have after the project is underway? Why does it matter to get scope boundaries right before work begins?
Key Teaching Points
  • Charter as a contract: It doesn't just describe the project -- it establishes the PM's authority with the PDT
  • Scope boundary: Funding year constraints affect scope; include fiscal year boundaries in the charter
  • Living document: Charter gets revisited at every major phase gate -- not set and forgotten
  • EM 5-1-11 ref: Chapter 2, PDT composition and PM authority
Common Misconceptions
  • Charter is just administrative paperwork (it's the PM's authority document)
  • PDT = project team (PDT is broader -- includes stakeholders who don't do the work)
Instructor Tip Ask one participant to read their charter scope statement aloud, then ask the room: "What could go wrong if that boundary isn't explicit?" This surfaces real scope creep scenarios from the Building 4521 context.
Transition to L2 (MP): "Charter establishes what we're doing and who's doing it. Next, [MP lead] will work through scope management -- how you define and control the what in detail."

Learning Objectives
  • Build a WBS that reflects the Building 4521 work package structure
  • Distinguish deliverable-based vs. activity-based decomposition
  • Connect WBS structure to later cost and schedule development
CF03 Facilitation

Tables build a first-draft WBS for Building 4521 on chart paper (or whiteboard). Give them 20 minutes. Run a gallery walk -- each table posts their WBS, groups rotate and annotate with sticky notes (questions or additions).

Debrief: surface the differences. What did one group include that others missed? What level of decomposition is too much?

At what WBS level does the work become manageable? What's the cost of going too deep -- or not deep enough?
Key Teaching Points
  • 100% rule: WBS must capture 100% of the work -- nothing out of scope in, nothing in scope out
  • WBS structure: Include inspection, design, procurement, construction, and turnover phases as distinct branches
  • Work packages vs. planning packages: Near-term work is decomposed to work packages; future work stays at planning package level
  • PMBOK ref: Ch. 7 Scope; EM 5-1-11 Appendix B
Instructor Tip If a group goes too activity-focused, ask: "Is that a deliverable or a task?" WBS is deliverable-oriented -- activities go in the schedule, not the WBS.
Watch For Groups often forget the PM management branch (PM labor, reviews, reporting). Make sure at least one group surfaces it so you can discuss why it belongs in the WBS.
Transition to L4 (MP): "You've decomposed the work. [MP lead] will show you how that WBS becomes the backbone of your schedule."

Learning Objectives
  • Select the appropriate estimating method for the project phase
  • Apply contingency and management reserve distinctions
  • Explain the Building 4521 cost structure to a non-technical stakeholder
CF05 Facilitation

Give teams the Building 4521 scope data and ask them to develop a rough order of magnitude (ROM) estimate using the method that fits the information available. Then push: "What would you need to do a parametric estimate? A definitive estimate?"

Surface the progression: ROM → Parametric → Definitive as project knowledge grows.

The project sponsor asks for a "firm" cost number at feasibility. What do you say, and how do you frame the uncertainty range?
Key Teaching Points
  • Estimate type drives accuracy: ROM is -25% to +75%, parametric narrows that range
  • Contingency vs. management reserve: Contingency is in the cost baseline; MR is above it -- different approvals required to use each
  • Cost drivers: Inflation, scope uncertainty, environmental unknowns, labor availability
  • PMBOK ref: Ch. 9 Cost; EM 5-1-11 cost guidance
Instructor Tip Ask: "Who owns the contingency?" Many PMs don't know. In USACE projects, contingency authority is often retained above the PM level. Surface this tension.
Transition to L6 (SF Fusion): "You've built scope, schedule, and cost baselines. Before we move to execution, [SF lead] will close out Day 2 through the Strengths lens."

Learning Objectives
  • Identify and score risks using probability x impact for Building 4521
  • Develop risk response strategies (avoid, transfer, mitigate, accept)
  • Distinguish between risk and issue management
CF08 Facilitation

Teams brainstorm risks for Building 4521, score them on a 5x5 matrix, and develop response strategies for the top 3. Then: compare matrices across tables. What did one group identify that others missed?

Push: "What's your risk threshold? At what probability x impact do you stop accepting and start mitigating?"

You've identified a risk that materializes as an issue mid-project. Walk me through the transition from your risk register to your issue log. What changes?
Key Teaching Points
  • Risk = uncertainty with consequence: Both positive and negative -- don't just list threats
  • Response strategies: Avoid (change plan), Transfer (insurance/contract), Mitigate (reduce P or I), Accept (conscious choice)
  • Risk categories: Subsurface conditions, funding rescission, regulatory changes, workforce availability
  • Risk owner vs. PM: Assign ownership; PM manages the register, not every risk response
Watch For Groups often conflate risk response with mitigation only. Make sure "accept" is discussed as a legitimate, conscious strategy -- not failure to plan.
Transition to L9 (SF Fusion): "Risk management is where your team's diverse strengths become a real advantage. [SF lead] will close Day 3 through that lens."

Learning Objectives
  • Reframe threats from L8 as potential opportunities
  • Apply positive risk response strategies (exploit, share, enhance, accept)
  • Build a PM mindset that scans for upside uncertainty, not just downside
CF11 Facilitation

Return to L8 risk registers. Ask teams: "Pick one threat. Can it also be an opportunity under different conditions?" Walk through the reframe process -- same uncertainty, different direction.

Example: Environmental discovery risk (threat: delay) can be reframe as opportunity if discovery enables scope reduction or future project pipeline.

What would it look like to build a team culture that actively looks for opportunities in project uncertainty -- not just threats to defend against?
Key Teaching Points
  • PMBOK 8th Ed framing: Risk includes both threats and opportunities -- not just negative events
  • Positive responses: Exploit (make it happen), Share (partner to capture), Enhance (increase probability), Accept (passive)
  • Opportunity applications: Phased work scoped to capture adjacent repairs; early access windows; contractor capacity availability
Instructor Tip This is often the lesson that shifts PM mindset most. Create space for the room to surface real examples from their own projects. The best discussions here come from the participants, not the case file.
Transition to L12 (SF Fusion): "You've reframed how you see project uncertainty. [SF lead] will connect that mindset shift to your individual strengths profile."
